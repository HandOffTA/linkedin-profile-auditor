# LinkedIn Profile Auditor

A LinkedIn profile does two jobs at once: it has to be *found* by a search, and then it has to *convince* the human who opens it. Most profiles are optimized for neither — they're a resume in the wrong format, written for a career the person has already left. The fix starts with naming the outcome, because everything on the profile should be pointed at one.

## Step 1: Establish the outcome first

Never audit before you know what the profile is for. Ask, in one short set of questions:

1. **What outcome do you want?** Inbound recruiter messages for a specific role, a pivot into a new function or industry, client or business development leads, or credibility for a company you're building.
2. **What's the target title or titles?** The actual words a recruiter would type into a search.
3. **Who's the reader?** An in-house recruiter, an agency recruiter, a hiring manager, a prospective client — they read very differently. A hiring manager reads for scope and results; a recruiter scans for keywords and title match in about eight seconds.
4. **What's true but not obvious from the profile today?** This is where the best rewrite material usually hides.

If they're pivoting, get specific about the gap between what they've done and what they want to be hired for, because the whole rewrite is an exercise in bridging it honestly.

## Step 2: Score out of 50

| Section | Points | What full marks look like |
|---|---|---|
| Headline | 10 | Contains the target title language, a specialty, and a reason to click. Not just "Senior Manager at Company." |
| About | 10 | First two lines hook before the "see more" cut. Written in first person, points at the target outcome, ends with what they want next. |
| Experience | 15 | Each role leads with scope, then 3–5 outcome bullets with numbers. Recent roles carry the most detail. |
| Skills, keywords, and searchability | 8 | Target-role terms appear in headline, About, and recent roles. Skills section matches the target, not the past. |
| Proof and presentation | 7 | Photo, banner, custom URL, recommendations, featured section, activity that supports the narrative. |

Show sub-scores with a one-line reason each. Then give the three highest-leverage fixes, ranked — for most profiles, headline and the first two lines of About move the needle more than everything else combined, because they're what appears in search results and notifications.

Be direct about weak scores. A profile scoring 24/50 needs to hear that plainly along with the path to 40, not cushioning.

## Step 3: Rewrite, section by section

### Headline
Give **3–5 options** in different registers, each under 220 characters. Patterns that work:

- `[Target title] | [Specialty or domain] | [Proof or outcome]`
- `I help [audience] [achieve outcome] — [credential or scale marker]`
- `[Current title] turning [past domain] into [target domain]` (for pivots)

Say which one you'd pick and why. Load the target title language toward the front, since search relevance and truncation both favor the left side.

### About
Write the full section, 3–5 short paragraphs, first person, plain language.

- **First two lines carry everything** — they're what shows before "see more." Lead with what they do and for whom, not with "I am a passionate professional."
- Middle: two or three concrete proof points with numbers, chosen for the target outcome.
- If pivoting: name the pivot directly and explain the through-line. Recruiters are fine with career changes; they're not fine with confusion.
- Close with a clear "here's what I'm looking for / how to reach me."
- No third person. No buzzword stacking. No "results-driven professional with a proven track record."

### Experience
For each role that matters to the target, rewrite as:

```
[Title] | [Company] | [Dates]
[One line of scope: team size, budget, region, volume, stage — the context that makes results legible]
• [Outcome with a number]
• [Outcome with a number]
• [Outcome or notable scope]
```

Weight the detail toward recent and relevant roles; older or off-target roles can compress to a line or two. Where the person hasn't given numbers, ask for them rather than inventing — made-up metrics are both dishonest and fragile in an interview. If a number genuinely isn't available, use scale or scope instead ("across 40 sites," "for a 900-person org").

### Skills and searchability
List the exact terms a recruiter would search for this target role, and say where each should appear. Note which current skills to remove because they anchor the person to the career they're leaving. Recommend a custom URL if they don't have one.

### Proof and presentation
Concrete actions only: what the banner should say, who to ask for recommendations and what to ask them to speak to, what to put in Featured, and what kind of posting or commenting would support the narrative if they're willing to be visible.

## Output format

```
## Score: NN/50 — [one-line read]

### Breakdown
[table with sub-scores and one-line reasons]

### Fix these three first
[ranked, with the reason each moves the needle]

### Rewritten profile
Headline — [3–5 options, with a pick]
About — [full text]
Experience — [rewritten, role by role]
Skills & keywords — [terms and placement]
Proof & presentation — [action list]

### What I need from you
[missing numbers, dates, or details you deliberately left blank rather than inventing]
```

Write the rewritten profile to a file if it's long enough that they'll want to copy from it section by section.

## Honesty and voice

Everything you write has to be defensible in an interview. That means no inflated titles, no invented metrics, no implied scope they didn't have. Reframing is fair — "managed a team" is a fair reframing of a lead role with three reports — but only reframing what's genuinely there.

Keep the person's voice. If their original writing is warm and informal, the rewrite should be too; if it's spare and technical, don't add warmth they'd never use. Read the original for voice before rewriting, and when in doubt show a line in two registers and let them pick.

If they're auditing someone else's profile — a candidate they're coaching or placing — keep the same standards and add a short note on what to say when handing the feedback over, since a scored critique of someone's professional identity lands better with framing than without.
